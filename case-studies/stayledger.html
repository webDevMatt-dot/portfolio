StayLedger

A shared record of your property, every stay. Guests check in, walk the inventory, flag problems with photos and check out — all from a link. Owners get a timestamped timeline of what was in the property, what changed and who said so.

The problem

Most Airbnb-style disputes come down to one question nobody can answer: was it like that when the guest arrived? Handover happens over WhatsApp, the inventory lives in the owner's head, and damage is noticed by the cleaner two days after check-out — when it's one person's word against another's.

The goal was a record both sides add to and neither side can quietly rewrite — and a guest flow light enough that people actually use it: no app to download, no account to create, just a link from the host.

What I built

Guest portal, no account

A link and an access code, then a one-time code to the email or phone on the booking. Wi-Fi, arrival instructions, the door code pinned and copyable, a guidebook, host and emergency contacts.

Guided check-in & check-out

Item-by-item walkthrough of the inventory with photos and notes on anything flagged, house rules acknowledged and timestamped, and a "before you leave" checklist with a signed declaration.

Inventory reconciliation

Areas and items with expected quantities, then expected vs counted at arrival vs counted at departure — so a missing towel is a line on a report, not an argument.

Issues during the stay

Guests can report a problem at any point, not just at the ends. Owners work it through a status workflow and their responses show up on the guest's side.

Owner dashboard & app

Properties with a status workflow, stays with generated access codes, check-in and check-out reports, structured reviews with per-category averages, and an Expo app for iOS and Android with native push.

An audit trail that holds

Every property has an append-only timeline, enforced by a database trigger rather than convention, plus alerts on check-in, new issues, check-out and reviews.

Design decision

Two auth models on purpose. Owners sign in through Supabase Auth and every query carries their session, so Postgres row-level security does the scoping — an owner can't read another owner's rows even if the app code has a bug. Guests never get an account: they get a signed, revocable session cookie scoped to one stay, and every guest query goes through a single data-access module that filters by the stay the session proves.

The harder parts

An access code isn't enough

Access codes get forwarded. So the code only gets a guest to the door of the flow — they then have to hold the email or phone the owner recorded on the booking. One-time codes are hashed with a pepper, expire in ten minutes, allow five attempts and are invalidated when a new one is issued. Wi-Fi passwords and the door code never reach a guest who hasn't passed that check.

A record neither side can edit

The timeline is only worth anything if it can't be rewritten after a dispute starts. Updates and deletes on the audit table raise an exception at the database level, so "append-only" is a property of the schema, not a promise in the code.

Designed for a guest at a door in the dark

The key-safe code started life buried in a paragraph of arrival instructions. It became its own field so it could be shown large, pinned to the screen and copied in one tap — the three things someone with a suitcase and a dying phone actually needs.

Two owner surfaces, one source of truth

The Expo app talks to the same Supabase project with the same anon key and the same row-level security as the web dashboard. There's no service-role key on the device and no bespoke API to keep in sync — the database is the contract. Guests stay on the web, which is the whole point of the link-and-OTP design.
