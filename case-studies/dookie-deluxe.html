Dookie Deluxe

A digestive-health tracker that turns a taboo daily habit into data people actually look at. Playful enough to open every morning, careful enough to hand a printout to a doctor.

The problem

Digestive conditions are extremely common and badly tracked. People living with IBS, Crohn's, colitis, coeliac disease or chronic constipation are routinely asked by a clinician to describe months of symptoms from memory — and so they end up keeping notes in a phone, a spreadsheet, or nowhere at all.

The data that would actually help is daily, boring, and slightly embarrassing to record. So the design problem isn't storage or analysis. It's getting someone to log honestly, every day, for months — and then turning that into something a GP can read in thirty seconds.

What I built

Two-speed logging

A one-tap quick log for the habit, and a full entry — Bristol scale, colour, difficulty, mood, notes — for anyone who wants the detail. The fast path is never gated.

Gut Regularity Score

A 0–100 score from regularity, consistency, hydration and recent symptoms, returned with a per-factor breakdown of points earned, points available and a specific tip.

Symptom checker

A question set graded into informational, see-a-GP and urgent, with red-flag logic covering blood, black stools, unexplained weight loss, persistent change in habit and night-time symptoms.

Calendar, timeline, analytics

A heatmap calendar, a chronological timeline, and trend analysis relating food, medication and symptoms to what actually happened.

Doctor-ready report

Generated on device and exportable, so a consultation starts from a record instead of "a few weeks, I think, maybe more".

Social layer

Streak leaderboards, a shareable year-in-review, and a shared calendar for a partner or GP — the loop that makes a daily habit stick and spreads the app without ads.

Getting the language right

This is the part of the project I'd defend hardest, and none of it is visible in a screenshot. A product that sits near health information can hurt people in two directions — by frightening someone who is fine, or by reassuring someone who should see a doctor.

The rename

The score started life as a Gut Health Score and became the Gut Regularity Score. It measures regularity, not health. The narrower claim is the more honest one — and it also happens to be the one that survives app-store review.

The wording rules are enforced as product requirements, not style preferences:

  • Medication effects say "may cause", never "often causes".
  • Risk messaging says "we've noticed a change in your recent logs that may be worth discussing with a healthcare professional" — never "you have".
  • The checker reports only the flags the user actually raised, and invents nothing.
  • Every educational article carries an explicit educational-only disclaimer.

Dookie Deluxe is a personal wellness product, not a medical device. It doesn't diagnose. Deciding that early, and writing the constraint into the copy rules rather than into a disclaimer nobody reads, is what keeps it defensible with users and reviewers alike.

    ├── expo-router          file-based routing, tab + auth groups
    ├── NativeWind           Tailwind syntax in React Native
    ├── TanStack Query       server cache, offline-tolerant reads
    └── Reanimated           gesture and transition work

  Device capabilities
    ├── expo-notifications      daily logging reminders
    ├── expo-local-authentication   biometric lock on health data
    ├── expo-image-picker       photo attachments
    ├── expo-print / sharing    doctor-ready PDF export
    └── expo-secure-store       tokens in the keychain, not storage

  Supabase   Postgres · auth · row-level security · storage
    └── social sign-in incl. Apple, via expo-auth-session

  EAS   build profiles → development · preview · production
    └── expo-updates  OTA JS delivery on channel per profile
Design decision

Health data gets a biometric lock and lives behind row-level security, and tokens go to the secure enclave rather than async storage. A bowel-movement log is the kind of thing people will only record honestly if they are certain nobody else will read it.

The harder parts

Shipping to testers in other countries

Early distribution ran through a local tunnel, which failed exactly as often as remote testers tried to use it. Moving to EAS with an organisation account fixed it: three build profiles on their own channels, standalone builds that check for updates on launch, and a JS-only change reaching every tester without a reinstall.

Knowing when OTA isn't enough

Over-the-air updates cover screens, logic, styling and new content. Native libraries, icon and splash changes, SDK upgrades and native config still need a rebuild. Getting that boundary wrong means publishing an update that silently does nothing — so the rule is written down rather than remembered.

Free tier as a growth mechanism

The monetisation model paywalls depth, health reporting and convenience — never the core logging loop or the sharing hook. A tracker that limits how often you can log destroys the habit it depends on, and a viral feature behind a paywall isn't viral.
